If you take one thing from this page, take this: an encumbrance certificate lists **registered** transactions only, and the Telangana Registration & Stamps Department says so in its own FAQ — "EC cannot be taken as a sole criterion to buy a property or lend money." A nil EC is a narrower result than most buyers assume, and the department's definition of it is worth reading in full before you treat one as comfort.

That is not a criticism of the certificate. It is a well-scoped document doing a well-scoped job, and the mistake is asking it a question it was never built to answer. This is general buyer education for plots in Telangana, not legal advice, and it is written so you can check every claim at the office that issues it.

## What an encumbrance certificate is

The department's FAQ defines it plainly. An encumbrance certificate is a record showing registered transactions pertaining to a property. When a mortgage, sale or any other deed relating to immovable property is registered, the Registration Department indexes it, and that indexing generates the certificate.

Two consequences follow directly from that definition, and both matter more than the fee:

- The certificate is a product of the **registration index**, not of the land record. The Revenue Department holds the pattadar passbook and the 1B extract; the Registration & Stamps Department holds the index of registered deeds. Separate systems, reading the same land.
- It is keyed to **the property description you supply**. The department's own disclaimer on its EC page is that "the encumbrances shown in the EC are those discovered with reference to the description of properties furnished by the applicants at the time of Registration." Search well, or you get a clean answer about the wrong property.

Confirm the current definition on the department's FAQ page at `registration.telangana.gov.in/faqsRegistration.htm` (question 38) before you rely on it.

## What "nil encumbrance" actually means

A nil EC means that no deeds were found registered against the property described in your application for the period searched. Here is the department's own wording, from FAQ question 39:

> "If Certificate is issued in this form, it means that there are no registered transactions / liabilities on the property for a given period of time; and unregistered transactions are not included in this certificate since such details are not available with Registration Department."

Read that second clause as the operative one. An unregistered sale deed, an oral agreement to sell, an unregistered family partition, a claim under a decree never indexed — none of these appear on a nil EC. Not because they do not exist, but because the Registration Department holds no record of them.

The same FAQ entry adds two further cautions worth carrying into any negotiation:

- **Fill the form properly.** The department warns that incomplete or inaccurate details in the EC application may produce a wrong certificate. The accuracy of the result is limited by the accuracy of your input.
- **You may inspect the records yourself.** An interested party may approach the Sub-Registrar and, on paying the prescribed fee, be permitted to verify the records personally.

A sensible working rule: a nil EC clears the plot against the registration index for the period you searched, and nothing else. Read it alongside the [pattadar passbook and 1B check](/blog/pattadar-passbook-1b-check/), a different system reading different records.

## How to run the search: three routes

The department's EC page lists three ways to search online. Under all three, selecting the district and the Sub-Registrar office is mandatory.

1. **Document number and year of document.** The most precise route, because it keys to a specific registered instrument rather than a described location. Use it when the seller can give the registration details of a known document, such as a prior sale deed or the parent's registered document in the chain.
2. **House number, old house number or apartment name**, with optional flat number and colony, locality or habitation. The department advises entering the house number in the standard ward, block and door or B.I. number format for better results.
3. **Survey number in a revenue village**, optionally with a plot number. For a plotted development this is usually the most useful route, because a plot is identified by its survey number and plot number in the approved plan.

Then pay the fee and download the online EC.

One honest warning from the department's own page: the search is probabilistic. Legacy property descriptions are not well structured or standardised, and the same property may be described differently over time as its classification changes. The result may return several entries, some of no interest to you. Select the relevant entry yourself, and do not assume the first result is the right one. Where the system returns "Data Not Found", the department's instruction is to approach the Sub-Registrar to confirm.

Run the search yourself, at `registration.telangana.gov.in`, before you pay anyone to run it for you.

## What it costs, and the instrument that fixes it

The fees come from the **Table of Registration Fees effective 02.09.2021**, notified under Section 78 of the Registration Act, 1908 (Act XVI of 1908):

- Encumbrance certificate for a search **up to 30 years: Rs.500 per certificate**.
- Encumbrance certificate for a search of **more than 30 years: Rs.1,000 per certificate**.
- Conducting search and issue of a certified copy: Rs.500 per certificate.

That table superseded the earlier 2013 fee structure. Fees are set by departmental order and are revised by further order, so confirm the figure in force on the day you pay — on the department's fee table and at the counter.

### Two errors you will meet online

Both are common enough to name.

**The stale 2013 figure.** Several live commercial pages still quote Rs.200 for a search up to 30 years and Rs.500 beyond it. That is the superseded 2013 table, roughly half the current figure, and the old order still sits in the department's own downloads folder. If a source quotes you Rs.200, ask which instrument it cites and check the date on it.

**The garbled age-based split.** A number of aggregator sites restate the two tiers as being about the _applicant's_ age — a different rate below 30 and another at 30 and above. That is wrong. The split in the gazette table is by the **search period**, that is, how far back you are asking the index to reach. It has nothing to do with the age of the person applying.

Also note the split is per certificate, not per year searched, and the period is the one you request rather than a fixed default.

## How far back the online search reaches

The department states that online EC is available for transactions from **01-01-1983 only**. Anything earlier is outside what the online system holds.

If the chain of title may run back before that date, the online search will not tell you whether there were transactions in that window, and you would need the jurisdictional Sub-Registrar. Treat this as a boundary of system coverage rather than a rule of law, and confirm the current cut-off on the department's EC page.

## MeeSeva and the Sub-Registrar: which counter does what

The department's own published material handles this least cleanly, so here is the position as it stands. The FAQ says encumbrance certificates are issued by "MeeSeva Centres and Sub Registrar and District Registrar Offices." However, later departmental instructions — Memo No. G1/13559/2012 dated 24.07.2012, and Government Memo No. 29588/Regn-I/A1/2012-3 dated 06.07.2013 — stopped the issue of encumbrance certificates and certified copies from Sub-Registrar offices in the remaining districts and moved delivery to the MeeSeva channel. The FAQ text appears not to have been revised to reflect that.

Practical guidance:

- **Plan around MeeSeva.** Centres are listed on the state's MeeSeva portal at `tg.meeseva.gov.in`, and a MeeSeva centre can tell you what it will accept and what it charges.
- **Confirm SRO availability before you travel.** The department's EC page says an ink-signed encumbrance certificate requires approaching the Sub-Registrar concerned. Whether your jurisdictional SRO currently issues one is something you should establish by telephone, not by assumption.
- **Ask about the service charge.** Neither the FAQ nor the fee table states whether MeeSeva adds a charge on top of the statutory fee. Ask at the centre and pay what is receipted.

We are not publishing a processing time for any route. No turnaround period is stated on the department's EC page or in the fee table. If a date matters to your transaction, ask the counter.

## What an encumbrance certificate does not prove

Stated plainly, because this is where certificates are most often over-read:

- **It does not prove title.** It does not tell you the seller owns the land, or has title to convey it. That comes from the pattadar passbook, the 1B extract and the chain of link documents.
- **It does not see unregistered dealings.** That is the department's own wording, quoted above.
- **It does not confirm land classification.** Assigned or prohibited land, a lake buffer, a nala corridor or road widening land is a Revenue Department question, answered through Bhu Bharati and the Mandal Revenue Officer.
- **It does not confirm approval.** It says nothing about a layout approval or a building permission for the parcel. See the [approvals guide](/blog/hmda-or-dtcp-plot-approval-telangana/).
- **It does not bind a lender.** The department says an EC cannot be the sole criterion to buy a property **or to lend money**. The [plot loans guide](/blog/plot-loans-telangana/) covers what a lender's legal team will ask for.
- **It is not conclusive against a conflict.** The department's page states that "in case of any conflict, original data shall prevail." The certificate is a reading of the record, and the record prevails.

The department's own recommendation is the right one: undertake a comprehensive study, using the property purchase precautions or the _Jagrattha_ short film referenced on its FAQ page, before you commit.

## Putting this into a transaction

1. **Get the survey number and plot number** from the approved plan, and the SRO jurisdiction for the village.
2. **Run the EC yourself** on the department's portal, across all three search modes if needed, and save the output.
3. **Pay for the certificate** at the current fee — Rs.500 up to 30 years, Rs.1,000 beyond — and confirm the figure in force that day.
4. **If the chain predates 01-01-1983**, go to the Sub-Registrar for the earlier period rather than relying on the online result.
5. **Give the result to your lawyer** as one document in the file, not as the file.
6. **Complete the rest of the checks** — revenue record, land classification, approvals, and the status of the specific plot on the approved plan.
