A plot loan looks like a home loan on the brochure, but three things work differently: the bank needs to believe you will build, the share it funds is its own commercial call rather than a rule, and the income-tax treatment only begins when a house exists. Each of those has a published rule behind it.

> **Reader note.** This is general buyer education, not tax or financial advice. Whether an interest deduction is available to you depends on your assessment year, the regime you are taxed under, and your lender's own terms. Confirm your position with a tax adviser before you rely on it.
>
> Last checked against the Reserve Bank of India's housing finance master circular, the Income Tax Department's Income-tax Act, 1961 section index, and the Income-tax Act, 2025 as published by the Income Tax Department, on 1 October 2026.

## The construction declaration is the entry condition

The Reserve Bank's housing-finance master circular sets the rule for land purchase in one line:

> Bank finance can be granted **only for purchase of a plot, provided a declaration is obtained from the borrower that he intends to construct a house on the said plot**, with the help of bank finance or otherwise, within such period as may be laid down by the banks themselves.

That is the dividing line. A plot loan is housing finance because you have declared you will build — within a deadline your lender sets. The loan you are offered for a plot you intend to hold as an asset is a different product with different pricing, if the lender offers it at all.

## How much a bank funds

The Loan-to-Value ceilings in the RBI's circular apply to housing loans, and the same circular tells banks how to compute the value they apply those ceilings to: **stamp duty, registration and other documentation charges must not be included in the property's cost** for LTV purposes, so the norm is not diluted.

What the circular does not do is hand a plot buyer the same share a house buyer gets. The ratio sanctioned on a bare plot is the lender's policy, and the plot's location, approvals and title are exactly the things that policy weighs. Ask two questions at the branch, in writing:

1. What percentage of the plot value will you fund, and on what valuation?
2. What is the EMI and tenure for **this** loan — not the home-loan illustration?

## The tax treatment starts later than most buyers expect

**First, a change you need to know about: the statute itself has changed.** The Income-tax Act, 1961 was **repealed with effect from 1 April 2026**. For income earned from that date the live provisions are in the **Income-tax Act, 2025**: the deduction for interest on borrowed capital is now **section 22(1)(b)** of the 2025 Act (with section 22(1)(c) covering the pre-construction period), and the default tax regime is now **section 202** — the successor to section 115BAC of the old Act. If you have read about this deduction in an article written before April 2026, that article was describing a repealed statute. Most online summaries have not caught up, so treat any page you find with a section number from the 1961 Act as history rather than law.

The substance has not changed in the way that matters to a plot buyer. Interest payable on capital borrowed to acquire or construct a property is deductible, computed under the head "income from house property". On a plot that is simply held, there is no house property to compute against — a plot with no building is not the kind of property the provision attaches to.

The section's Explanation then sets how the interest spent on the plot lands: interest payable for the period **before** the year in which the property is acquired or constructed is deductible **in equal instalments for that year and each of the four succeeding years** — five instalments, starting when the house arrives.

The widely quoted ₹2 lakh figure needs care twice over, because it is the number most often copied and the number most often misread.

**First, one of its conditions has already fallen away.** The 1961 Act framed the ceiling around a loan taken "on or after 1 April 1999" and construction completed within five years from the end of the financial year in which the capital was borrowed. The 1999 date was a condition of the **repealed** Act; it does not appear in section 22 of the 2025 Act, which carries two conditions instead — construction completed within five years of the end of the tax year in which the capital was borrowed, and a certificate from the lender. Miss either and the ceiling for self-occupied property falls to ₹30,000. The "after April 1999" test you may have read quoted as current law is not.

**Second, it belongs to the old regime.** The ceiling is part of the rules that section 202 of the 2025 Act — section 115BAC before it — disallows by default. Under the default regime a self-occupied property carries no deduction for interest: the house-property loss cannot be set off against any other income, and the claim on the self-occupied case is treated as unavailable. So a buyer reading the ₹2 lakh figure as their tax outcome is reading the wrong column:

|                                                      | Old regime                                                                     | Default regime under section 202 (formerly 115BAC)                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Interest on a self-occupied property                 | Deductible, subject to the section's conditions, including the ₹2 lakh ceiling | Not available                                                                                  |
| Loss from that property set off against other income | Allowed                                                                        | Not allowed                                                                                    |
| What has to happen for the left-hand column          | —                                                                              | You have to opt out of the default regime, and take the slab rates that apply without the rebate |

**Third, it is an aggregate, not a per-property allowance.** The ₹2 lakh is a ceiling on the deduction for self-occupied property interest in aggregate. The Income Tax Department states this directly: for two self-occupied house properties you may claim an aggregate deduction of up to ₹2,00,000, not ₹2,00,000 each. Holding two self-occupied properties does not give you two ceilings. If your facts involve more than one property, that reading changes the answer, so have it checked rather than assumed.

Both points need a caveat we would rather state than hide. The law here changed on 1 April 2026 and the section numbers below follow the 2025 Act; the wording that matters to you is the wording in force for **your** assessment year, and the numbering of a new statute's provisions is exactly the kind of thing summaries get wrong. The description above is written for tax years from 1 April 2026, when section 202 applies by default. Treat the table as the reason to go and ask a tax adviser, not as a substitute for asking one.

On where to read it: we used to link a dated page reproduction of section 24 of the 1961 Act. A snapshot of a living section is not the current text, and that section no longer exists. Read the **Income-tax Act, 2025** as published by the Income Tax Department instead, and check the section numbering against the consolidated text rather than against an article.

The practical consequence: keep every interest certificate from the lender from day one. The paperwork for a plot loan is the paperwork for a claim that may begin years later — and, under the default regime, only if you build.

## The tax you meet at purchase is not the interest deduction

Everything above concerns a deduction you may claim years later, and only if you
build. There is a second tax charge that lands on the day you buy, and it arrives
whether or not you take a loan.

When you buy immovable property, tax is deducted at source by the buyer. Under the
Income-tax Act, 2025 that obligation sits in **section 393(1)**, at the Table entry
for transfer of immovable property other than agricultural land. The rate is **1%**,
and the base is the consideration **or** the stamp duty value of the property,
whichever is **higher** — so the agreement price and the valuation used for duty
are both in scope, and the higher one counts. Where both the consideration and the
stamp duty value of a property are **below Rs. 50 lakh**, no deduction is due on
that property. The threshold is framed per property, so how several purchases in one
tax year are then treated is a question for your tax adviser.

This was **section 194-IA of the repealed Income-tax Act, 1961** before 1 April 2026.
Most material still in circulation quotes that section number. Confirm the section,
the base and the rate in section 393 as enacted on the Income Tax Department portal
before you rely on it, and take the filed challan-cum-statement from the
sub-registrar who records the transaction.

One further numbering trap, relevant whenever a plot bought on loan is later sold:
the mode of computation of capital gains under the 2025 Act is **section 72**, not
section 48. Section 48 of the 2025 Act is a different provision, on tea, coffee and
rubber development accounts; section 48 was the computation provision under the
1961 Act. If a computation reaches for section 48, check which Act it was written
against. Buying on loan changes that arithmetic anyway, because the interest is a
cost of holding rather than a cost of purchase.

## What the lender checks about the plot — and why approvals decide the loan

The legal check on a plotted purchase runs through the same facts a buyer should check, and one of them can end the application outright:

- **The layout's approval.** Which authority approved it, and the approval number. A Gram Panchayat layout without technical approval from the town planning directorate or the urban development authority is not a valid layout — the state's regularization FAQ says so in one word.
- **Whether the plots can be registered at all.** A plot the sub-registrar will not register is a plot the bank will not take as security, and registration of plots by sale in an unapproved layout is not permitted — the rule and its sources are in [what cannot be registered](/blog/plot-registration-telangana-costs/). Approval comes before financing, in that order.
- **The project's RERA registration**, where it applies — confirm the number on the Telangana RERA portal and match the project name and location.
- **Title, link documents and the encumbrance certificate** — the same chain a lawyer reviews for you.
- **The specific plot.** Lenders value the plot on the approved plan, so parcels marked mortgage or reserved are not what they will lend against.

The [approvals guide](/blog/hmda-or-dtcp-plot-approval-telangana/) explains how to establish which authority had jurisdiction, and the [registration guide](/blog/plot-registration-telangana-costs/) covers the documents and the valuation mechanics.

## Before you apply

- Enquire whether your lender will consider **that exact plot**, not the project in general — the answer is often specific to the development.
- Ask for a **composite plot-plus-construction loan** if you plan to build; it is the structure the interest treatment attaches to.
- Keep the approval letter and layout plan ready from the developer, alongside your own documents.
- Confirm the **government market value** the registration will run on — the charges and the loan valuation do not have to be the same number.
